Saffron is among the most adulterated ingredients traded anywhere — cut with safflower, dyed marigold, bulked with style thread, or simply mislabelled as to origin.

The three markers

ISO 3632 is the international standard for saffron. It measures three compounds by spectrophotometry, and together they are difficult to fake: crocin for colouring strength, picrocrocin for taste, and safranal for aroma.

Category I — the highest grade — requires a colouring strength of 190 or above. Every consignment we take is tested by an independent laboratory before it is accepted, and the certificate is tied to the batch number.

How saffron gets faked

It helps to know what you are avoiding. The common adulterations are:

  • Safflower or marigold dyed red and sold as saffron thread. Cheap, and obvious under a lab test.
  • Style thread — the pale yellow-white section below the stigma. It has little colour or aroma but adds weight, which is why grading matters.
  • Colouring agents added to weak saffron to lift the apparent crocin reading.
  • Origin substitution — saffron grown elsewhere, packed and sold as Kashmiri.

Grade 1 all-red coupe means the style has been removed entirely. That is the grade we buy, and the reason our yield per kilogram of flower is lower than it could be.
